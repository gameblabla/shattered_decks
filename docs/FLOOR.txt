• Yes—a small, flexible textured 3D renderer targeting 5 fps is plausible on MSX2. But texturing the entire current
  board, with all cards and unrestricted camera movement, would need compromises. Delta drawing helps most when the
  camera is stationary; it cannot guarantee that frame rate during rotation.

  RT3D demonstrates the relevant approach: transform shared vertices, reject invisible faces, use painter ordering,
  then texture horizontal spans through CPU writes to SCREEN 8. Turbor describes affine texture stepping without
  perspective correction. The VDP handles solid fills and copies; it does not perform texture mapping. RT3D author’s
  explanation.

  Those figures exclude geometry, clipping, row setup, texture loading, music, and other game work. The current
  chair-view arena covers roughly 18,000 pixels including its walls. Texturing all that already consumes almost the
  entire budget under optimistic conditions.

  There is also a limitation in the existing card mapper (src/msx2/msx2_raster.c): its fast loop walks one texture
  row per screen row. It does not interpolate arbitrary U and V across a span. A rotating floor generally needs
  both. We can reuse its output loop techniques, texture caching, and fixed-point stepping, but a flexible mapper
  needs a new general path—and its cost must be measured rather than assumed to remain 39 T/pixel.

  The delta approach has three distinct opportunities:

  - Stationary camera: excellent. Retain the textured board and repaint only card footprints and changed effects. A
    textured background then costs nothing most frames.

  - Camera translation or rotation: much weaker. Pixels inside an unchanged polygon outline can sample different
    texels. The flat-color delta counts in my plan therefore do not carry over to textures.

  - Occlusion: useful even during motion. Determine which floor spans cards cover before sampling the floor, then
    draw only visible surfaces. This saves both texture calculations and VRAM writes.

  Comparing newly rendered pixels with old pixels only saves transfers after paying to generate the new pixels.
  Reading the old screen back from VRAM adds more work, while two RAM copies of the arena require about 58 KiB—well
  beyond this port’s current working allocation. Compact descriptions of unchanged surfaces are a better starting
  point than full pixel comparison.

  I would design the engine around these constraints:

  1. Runtime geometry with shared vertices, fixed-point transforms, clipping, and bounded polygon counts. No per-
     camera bitmap assets.

  2. Two texture paths: a specialized floor/card path where the projection permits it, plus a general affine
     triangle path for arbitrary orientations.

  3. Visibility before texturing, avoiding hidden floor and repeated overdraw.
  4. Small reusable textures and mip levels. Distant material becomes coarse or flat; walls can remain solid. Four
     16×16 textures cost only 1 KiB before optional variants.

  5. Per-page retained state, with conservative invalidation when camera or texture mapping changes.
  6. A measured texture budget, initially around 8,000–10,000 visible textured pixels per moving frame, shared
     between board and cards. This is a prototype target, not a demonstrated limit.

  Merely enlarging texels does not eliminate the cost of writing their destination pixels. Coarser textures help
  when they produce long same-color runs that can become HMMV fills, or when they reduce sampling work enough to
  matter.

  My recommendation is to pursue 5 fps with a textured floor, solid walls, distance-based detail, and live geometry.
  That can be flexible and economical in ROM. The decisive prototype is an oblique rotating view with populated
  cards and music—not an empty stationary board. It should establish whether full-resolution texturing fits, or
  whether motion needs reduced detail.
